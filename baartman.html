Portrait Print of Sarah Baartman (1810)

Historical Portrait Print of Sarah Baartman (Source: British Museum, 2022; Ref: 1917,1208.3712)

Historical Description & Context

Sarah Baartman was a Khoikhoi woman born around 1789 near the Gamtoos River in the Eastern Cape (Crais & Scully, 2009:18). Taken to Europe under deceptive contracts in 1810, she was exhibited in London and Paris as the "Hottentot Venus" under conditions of extreme racial and gendered exploitation (Qureshi, 2004:234; Holmes, 2007). Graphic prints, aquatints, and caricatures produced during this era reflect the scientific racism and imperial gaze dominant in 19th-century Britain and Europe (Davids, 2018:45).

This specific portrait print is preserved within the Department of Prints and Drawings at the British Museum (Registration Number: 1917,1208.3712) (British Museum, 2022). Bequeathed by Lady Charlotte Schreiber in 1917, the work depicts Baartman in full length, wearing costume elements associated with her public exhibitions in London (British Museum, 2022). Such holdings in imperial museum collections illustrate how visual culture was deployed to construct racialized narratives in 19th-century Britain (Qureshi, 2004:240).

Following South Africa's democratic transition, President Nelson Mandela formally requested the repatriation of her remains from the Musée de l'Homme in Paris (Cruywagen, 2010:12). In May 2002, her remains were buried at Hankey in the Gamtoos Valley, establishing her memory as an enduring symbol of indigenous identity, dignity, and anti-colonial restitution (Republic of South Africa, 2002).
